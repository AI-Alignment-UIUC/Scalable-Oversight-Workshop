01 · Iterated amplification

Distill the team into X, then amplify again

Step 1

Amplify

Run H plus copies of X on sampled questions. Record the answers.

Step 2

Distill

Train X by supervised learning to give the team’s answers in one fast pass.

Step 3

Repeat

The team gets stronger because X did. X chases a moving target.

The idealized limit, HCH: “Humans Consulting HCH”, a tree of overseers each able to consult more trees.

The bet, factored cognition: hard reasoning breaks into small pieces a human can check.

Distillation averages away independent noise and faithfully copies consistent bias into every answer built on it. In SRC criterion terms: random error is contracting, systematic error is compounding.

02 · Debate

Two agents argue. A judge decides.

“A question q ∈ Q is shown to both agents. The two agents state their answers a₀, a₁ ∈ A. The two agents take turns making statements s₀, s₁, . . . , sₙ₋₁ ∈ S. The judge sees the debate (q, a, s) and decides which agent wins.”

Irving, Christiano & Amodei (2018), AI Safety via Debate, §2

The judge stands in for a human who could never solve the question alone. The debaters stand in for strong models.

02 · The bet

“In the debate game, it is harder to lie than to refute a lie.”

Irving, Christiano & Amodei (2018), §2

Alice names a midpoint. Bob picks the half he thinks is false. A false claim always has a false half, so a strong Bob drives it down to one checkable step.

“For any problem L ∈ PSPACE, we can choose a polynomial time judge so that optimal play in the debate game with polynomially many steps solves L.” Irving et al., §2.2

Notebook 3 · the pixel game

A judge that sees four pixels

The judge sees only a few pixels of a digit. Two debaters see it all, and take turns choosing which pixels to reveal: one argues for the true label, one for a lie.

“With 6 pixels, debate with precommit turns a 59.4% accurate judge into a 88.9% accurate debate.”

Irving et al. (2018), §3.1, on MNIST

31%

our judge alone, 4 random pixels of an 8×8 digit

70%

debate, honest player beats every possible lie

The honest player often reveals a blank pixel. “There’s no ink here” is evidence too.

02 · What can go wrong

Flaws that are hard to find

The authors’ own worry · §5.2

“Will one of the agents be able to sneak in a statement that appears logical but contains a hidden flaw without the other agent pointing out the flaw?”

Obfuscated arguments · Barnes 2020

“a dishonest debater can often create arguments that have a fatal error, but where it is very hard to locate the error.”

Exploration

A debater that hasn’t learned to find flaws lets the liar through (Notebook 2).

Judges

Real judges tire, have biases, and can be persuaded by style.

03 · Recursive reward modeling

Assistants help you evaluate

“In step k we use the agent Aₖ₋₁ to assist the user in evaluating outcomes when training agent Aₖ.”

Leike et al. (2018), Scalable agent alignment via reward modeling, §3.2

Their example

Evaluating a novel, where “the user is assisted by an agent that provides auxiliary input: extracting a summary of the plotline, checking spelling and grammar, summarizing character development.”

The bet

Evaluating is easier than doing, and assistance scales with each round.

03 · The open question

“An important open question is whether errors accumulate: do the mistakes of the more narrow agent Aₖ₋₁ lead to larger mistakes in the training of agent Aₖ?”

Leike et al. (2018), §3.2

Notebook 1’s answer: it depends on whether the errors are random or systematic. Notebook 4’s: it depends on what nobody evaluates.
