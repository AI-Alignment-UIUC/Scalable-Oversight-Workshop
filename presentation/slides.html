01 · Method

Distill the team into X, then amplify again

Step 1 · §2.2

Amplify

Sample a question, let H answer it with copies of X, and “record every decision made by H during the process.”

Step 2 · §2.2

Model the human

“We train a model H′ to predict the decisions made by H in each of these transcripts”

Step 3 · §2.2

Distill

Amplify again with H′ in place of H. “X is trained by supervised learning on these (Q, A) pairs.”

No outside objective: “the objective is implicit in the way that the human coordinates the copies of X” (§1.1)

Repeat: the amplified team “is modestly smarter than X working on its own, and X chases this moving target” (§2.3)

02 · Debate · Method

Two agents argue. A judge decides.

“A question q ∈ Q is shown to both agents. The two agents state their answers a₀, a₁ ∈ A (which may be the same). The two agents take turns making statements s₀, s₁, . . . , sₙ₋₁ ∈ S. The judge sees the debate (q, a, s) and decides which agent wins.”

Irving, Christiano & Amodei (2018), AI Safety via Debate, §2

The judge stands in for a human who could never solve the question alone. The debaters stand in for strong models.

02 · The claim

“In the debate game, it is harder to lie than to refute a lie.”

02 · The paper’s own worries

Ten reasons to worry (§5)

  • Maybe humans believe whatever they want to hear?
  • Will humans understand the debates?
  • Is honesty actually the best policy?
  • Will agents trained for debate lose performance?
  • Uncertainty about the neighborhood around Nash equilibrium
  • Are equilibria stable during training?
  • Lack of debate convergence
  • Security vulnerabilities in the human or code
  • Stochastic simulation
  • Training superhuman AI to be convincing sounds dangerous!

“Will one of the agents be able to sneak in a statement that appears logical but contains a hidden flaw without the other agent pointing out the flaw?”

§5.2, Will humans understand the debates?

03 · What the paper found

A frozen reward model gets exploited

“Over time the agent learns to exploit the reward model: the perceived performance (according to the reward model) increases, while the actual performance (according to the game score) plummets.”

Fig. 4, reproduced from Ibarz et al. (2018), Atari

Reward hacking · §4.3

“an effect that lets the agent get more reward than intended by exploiting loopholes in the process determining the reward”

The fix · §5.1 Online feedback

“If we provide the agent with reward feedback online, we get a tighter feedback loop between the user’s feedback and the agent’s behavior.”
